Decision: Melete can get the certificate for its own HTTPS from an ACME CA (Let's Encrypt) and renew it, off until set up as an outside service. The domain is proved by the DNS challenge through deSEC, so the certificate is a wildcard with the bare name; the CA's staging server is used until an admin switches to the real one. The account's key is made from the OS's randomness and kept sealed, one account per CA directory, and its URI is shown for a CAA accounturi record. The certificate and its key are written to the config folder, where HTTPS reads them and takes a new pair up at once; certificate files given in the environment are refused beside it. It is renewed in the window the CA's renewal information suggests, else two-thirds through its life, with retries further and further apart kept across restarts, and a renewal that fails alerts admins (again at most every three days while it fails), saying when the certificate in use runs out, in fixed words of Melete's own (the CA's and deSEC's own text stays on the Certificates page). Asking for a certificate now is refused while one is being got, for 15 minutes after the last request, and while the certificate in use is less than a day old and from the same CA for the same names, and it doesn't skip the wait once attempts keep failing; a renewal time is kept between a tenth of the certificate's life (a day at most) after its start and a third of it (three days at most) before its end, whatever window the CA suggests. What's held counts only if the certificate and key load as a pair. deSEC changes a record set whole, so another client changing the same _acme-challenge record at the same moment can lose its change; Melete reads its own back and fails if it was lost, and notes its values before setting them so that values a crash left are taken out at the next look, never anyone else's.

Why: A household passing HTTPS through a VPS then needs nothing else at home to get and renew a certificate, and the certificate never sits on the VPS. With a wildcard, Certificate Transparency logs don't list the name Melete is reached at, and the DNS challenge needs nothing opened at home. Staging first keeps first tries clear of the real server's rate limits. The CA no longer sends expiry emails, so the alert is what warns before HTTPS stops working. Each certificate asked for counts against the CA's limits on duplicates, so nothing an admin clicks, and no window a CA suggests, can use them up and block a real renewal.
